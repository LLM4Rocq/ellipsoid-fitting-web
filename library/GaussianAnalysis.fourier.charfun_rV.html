For a law nu on 'rV[R]_m, cf_rV nu theta is the characteristic function of nu at theta, given by its real and imaginary parts, the integrals of cos <x, theta> and sin <x, theta> against nu; it is the characteristic function at 1 of the law of <x, theta> (cf_rV_law). It is continuous in theta and of modulus at most one. The characteristic function of gauss m is exp (- |theta|^2 / 2) (cf_rV_gauss). The law of the sum of independent vectors has the complex product of their characteristic functions (cf_rV_add), and the law of \sum_k x_k w_k, for independent real x_k, has the complex product of those of the x_k at <w_k, theta> (cf_rV_tuple_prod).

Gaussian regularization, as in the method of Ball and Nazarov: for Y of law nu and an independent G of law gauss m, E[exp (- |z - Y|^2 / (2 s^2))] is a Gaussian average of cf nu (cf_rV_regularization), hence at most (s / sqrt (2 pi))^m times the integral of |cf nu| exp (- s^2 |theta|^2 / 2) (cf_rV_regularization_le), and P(Y + s G in B) is at most (2 pi)^-m times that integral times the Lebesgue measure of B (prod_gauss_le_cf_rV). No Fourier inversion is needed. The law of y + s G has the density w |-> s^-m gauss_pdf ((w - y) / s) (ge0_integral_gauss_affine). Letting s tend to 0: if |cf nu| is integrable, a closed ball has probability at most (2 pi)^-m (\int |cf nu|) times its Lebesgue measure (ball_le_cf_rV).

Here <x, theta> = mxdot x theta and |x|^2 = mxdot x x.
